\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

How much of a solid dissolves in a given liquid decides whether a drug is formulated as a tablet or
an injection, which solvent a crystallisation runs in, and how a product is purified. The value is
measured one solute, one solvent and one temperature at a time. Most combinations of industrial
interest have therefore never been measured, and a laboratory that needs one is choosing between
running an experiment and trusting a prediction. The same is true across process chemistry, where
a solvent is selected for a separation long before anyone measures the relevant phase behaviour.

Two kinds of prediction are on offer. A direct model regresses solubility from molecular
descriptors or from a representation learned off the molecular graph, and returns a number. A
physics-informed model predicts the quantities a thermodynamic argument names, then combines them
through a fixed relation taken from that argument. The second kind has become popular for reasons
that have little to do with accuracy, on which it usually trails
\cite{osanloo2024,vermeire2022solprop}. Its internal quantities are things chemistry already knows
how to measure and tabulate, so a prediction can be taken apart and checked piece by piece. Each
piece can be trained against reference data that a direct regressor has no way to use. And the
fixed relation supplies, for free, structure that a purely statistical model would have to learn
from data it does not have, which is the situation whenever the molecule in question resembles
nothing in the training set.

That design rests on an assumption so natural it is rarely written down. If the quantity inside the
model is a real physical quantity, then a better value for it can be looked up and handed to the
model, and the prediction should improve. The assumption is what makes the internal quantity worth
having, because it is the difference between a model that can be audited and repaired piece by
piece and an elaborate curve fit with suggestive variable names. It also carries direct practical
weight: it is the reason a laboratory would pay for a quantum-chemical calculation on a new
compound, or curate a table of measured melting properties, rather than spend the same effort on
more solubility measurements.

The quantity this work examines is the $\sigma$-profile. In the conductor-like screening model for
real solvents and in its segment-activity reformulation COSMO-SAC \cite{lin2002}, a molecule enters
the thermodynamics only through the distribution of screening-charge density over its cavity
surface, that is, through how much of that surface carries charge density of each value. Everything
else about the compound is discarded. The distribution comes from a quantum-chemical calculation
with a continuum solvation treatment and is tabulated once per molecule \cite{mullins2006}. It is
an unusually attractive thing to learn: it is a fixed-length vector, it is tabulated for thousands
of compounds, and supplying it from structure removes the only expensive step in the pipeline.

Group-contribution schemes supplied it first \cite{mu2007gc,mu2009gc}, and learned predictors
followed, taking the profile from a line notation or a molecular graph
\cite{kang2022surrogate,chen2021transformer,zhang2022mpnn}. More recent work embeds the learning
deeper, replacing both the profile and the segment-activity calculation with networks trained
inside the thermodynamic structure, and reports accuracy beyond the model it is built from
\cite{yang2025tennet}. Others keep the thermodynamics and dispense with the named intermediate
altogether \cite{specht2024hanna}, or learn a thermodynamic potential directly from phase
behaviour. Across this literature the internal quantity is described as physically meaningful on
the grounds that it appears in the thermodynamic formulation. Validation, where it is reported for
the intermediate at all, compares averaged predicted profiles against averaged reference profiles,
or rests on the accuracy of the final prediction.

What has not been asked is whether the values a network places in that slot are the physical
quantity. The question is not rhetorical. A solubility measurement constrains the combination of
the intermediate with the fixed relation, not the intermediate on its own, so different internal
values can reproduce the same measurements equally well; the directions the data leave undetermined
are where a flexible network is free to move. Machine learning has met this before under a different name. In models that predict human-meaningful concepts and then predict the target from the concepts alone, the concepts leak. They carry information their labels do not name, and interventions on them behave in ways the names do not predict
\cite{koh2020cbm,margeloiu2021,mahinpei2021,havasi2022}. The thermodynamic case differs in one
respect that makes it tractable. A $\sigma$-profile is not a human label but a computable quantity,
so the leak can be measured against the quantity itself.

This work measures it. We examine a pipeline that predicts the solubility of a solid through
COSMO-SAC, with a graph network supplying the $\sigma$-profile, trained both against tabulated
reference profiles and through the closure. Solubility data do not determine the quantities the
model composes. The network places screening-charge density where the reference tabulation carries none, and
does so preferentially in the windows the hydrogen-bonding term reads; that departure turns out to
be the most visible and the least consequential of those reported here. The output layer is not the
cause: the same network reaches far smaller values elsewhere in the same profile, and an untrained
network of the same architecture is worse by more than an order of magnitude. A molecule's profile
depends on whether it enters as solute or as solvent, so the predicted activity of a molecule in
itself departs from unity. What misplaced density costs the prediction varies by more than an order
of magnitude along the charge-density axis and steps at the hydrogen-bonding threshold. The learned
profile does not decompose: replacing part of it with the reference values is frequently worse than
replacing none. And the error that reaches the thermodynamics lives in the magnitude of the profile
where it is populated, not in the spurious density where it should be empty.
