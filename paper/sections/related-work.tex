\section{Related work} \label{sec:related}

Two families of methods now dominate solid solubility and solvation prediction, and they make opposite bets about physical structure. Physics-informed pipelines such as SolProp \cite{vermeire2022solprop} assemble a thermodynamic cycle from separately learned components (solvation free energy, vapor pressure, fusion properties), so that each prediction is decomposable and the intermediate quantities carry physical meaning. Direct models forgo that structure: FastSolv \cite{osanloo2024} regresses solubility from molecular descriptors with a descriptor-based deep neural network (fastprop), and it is the current leader on new-solute extrapolation, a task on which inter-laboratory disagreement sets a substantial irreducible-error floor. The ordering is unambiguous on accuracy: the black box leads, and the physics-informed cycle trails it. Our work does not try to overturn this ordering. It asks instead where the ceiling of the physics prior comes from, and it uses a matched direct control that shares our encoder so that the accuracy gap is attributable to the closure rather than to representation.

The activity coefficient is the term our closure must supply, and learned surrogates for it have advanced quickly. Winter et al. \cite{winter2022} predict infinite-dilution activity coefficients \lngi\ directly from SMILES with a language model, and Sanchez-Medina et al. \cite{sanchezmedina2023} embed a Gibbs-Helmholtz constraint in a graph network to capture the temperature dependence of \lngi. These treat \lng\ as the regression target. We take the complementary route of retaining a mechanistic activity model, either COSMO-SAC over a predicted $\sigma$-profile or NRTL, as a differentiable closure inside the solver, so that the network predicts physical inputs rather than the coefficient itself. That choice is what exposes the failure mode we measure: the misspecification of a fixed mechanistic map can dominate the insufficiency of its learned inputs.

Beyond solubility, the question we pose---when a fixed physical structure helps or hurts a learned model---is the central tension of physics-informed machine learning \cite{karniadakis2021}. Its dominant instantiations impose structure as a differentiable object: physics-informed neural networks enforce a governing equation through the loss \cite{raissi2019}, while physics-guided and gray-box hybrids compose learned components with fixed mechanistic maps \cite{willard2022}---exactly our $g(h(x))$, with $g$ the mechanistic map. A recent operator-learning line even adds a trainable correction to a \emph{misspecified} governing equation \cite{ma2026}; our concern is orthogonal to such constructions---not how to correct the map, but when the learned correction it induces is a compensating surrogate that forfeits the interpretability the structure was meant to buy. The prevailing expectation is that more physics improves generalization, yet the same structure can degrade it: physics-informed networks have documented failure modes in which the imposed constraint makes optimization or accuracy worse \cite{krishnapriyan2021}.

The closest analogue to the mechanism we isolate is in neural PDE solvers: Mohan et al.\ \cite{mohan2024} show that a learnable term added to a fixed discretized operator absorbs the simulation's Taylor-truncation artifacts rather than the physics, so the differentiable program's apparent interpretability is illusory. Their error source is \emph{numerical} and they have no reference for the learned term; the affordance we exploit---an external oracle for the intermediate (VT-2005 $\sigma$-profiles, tabulated Hammett constants)---is what turns that qualitative illusion into a \emph{measured, decomposable, sign-predicted} effect, and lets us read the fixed map's Jacobian for the low-rank under-determination behind the compensating surrogate (\S\ref{sec:surrogate}) rather than, as they do, for numerical stability. Our contribution follows from that one affordance and does two things: we make that degradation \emph{measurable} (decomposing it into closure misspecification and input insufficiency, with a sign rule for when a fixed-closure prior must lose), and the \emph{same} grounding exposes the learned intermediate, held against its reference value, as a closure-compensating surrogate---so the bottleneck's apparent interpretability is not guaranteed where the closure is misspecified.

This compensation is the physics-closure analogue of \emph{concept leakage} in concept-bottleneck models, where a learned concept silently encodes information beyond its nominal meaning and the ``interpretation'' is misleading \cite{mahinpei2021,margeloiu2021,havasi2022}. Recent work already traces one route to leakage to the \emph{downstream} model rather than to a trainable head exploiting spare capacity: an inflexible or misspecified head forces the encoder to deform the concept into carrying the dependence the head cannot represent \cite{parisini2025}, and overwriting a \emph{frozen} head's leaked concepts with their ground-truth values can then \emph{degrade} accuracy \cite{espinosa2025leakage}. Our setting is that route in its cleanest, most extreme form---the downstream map is a \emph{fixed physical operator}, uncorrectable even in principle, so the encoder must absorb its misspecification---and the external oracle adds two things absent from that literature: it turns leakage from a diagnosis into a \emph{measurement} against a reference value, and it reveals the leak to be \emph{structured}, low-rank and transferable across chemistry rather than the diffuse, per-example or scalar-information object of CBM-leakage measurement. The solubility closure is our worked instance; the pKa closure and synthetic families show the account is not tied to it.

We borrow several diagnostic lenses and claim no new theory from them. The gap between a fixed physical map and reality is the model-discrepancy problem of Kennedy and O'Hagan \cite{kennedy2001} and its calibration critique \cite{brynjarsdottir2014}; separating whether predictions are right on average from whether they are sharp is the calibration refinement and reliability-resolution decomposition of Murphy and DeGroot \cite{murphy1973,degroot1983}; fitting a wrong model still converges to a well-defined pseudo-true limit under the quasi-maximum-likelihood account of White \cite{white1982}. That the decomposition is only weakly constrained by solubility alone is a matter of parameter sloppiness \cite{transtrum2015,gutenkunst2007} and of structural versus practical identifiability \cite{raue2009,villaverde2016}. Finally, the risk that apparent skill reflects pipeline choices rather than physics is the concern of underspecification \cite{damour2022}, leakage and reproducibility \cite{kapoor2023}, and compositional risk \cite{mahajan2025crm}. Our data come from BigSolDB 2.0 \cite{krasnov2025bigsoldb}.

Positioned against this literature, our contribution is not a new model and not an accuracy result but a \emph{map}---organized by closure fidelity and latent supervision---of when grounding a fixed physical closure on an externally referenced latent helps or hurts, reconciling our worse-with-reference-inputs result with the supervised-$\sigma$ models that report the opposite. FastSolv and SolProp remain the reference points for accuracy, which we do not contest.
