\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

Table~\ref{tab:battery} lists every measurement reported below together with the control it is read
against. The order follows the question rather than the chronology: first whether the data
determine the internal quantity at all, then what the network does with the freedom that remains,
then what that costs the thermodynamics.

\subsection{Solubility does not determine the quantities the model composes}
\label{sec:ident}

Equation~\eqref{eq:sle} is a sum of two terms, and a solubility measurement constrains the sum. The
question is whether a set of such measurements separates them. For a fit of the four parameters
that govern the crystal term and the temperature dependence of the activity coefficient, the
information a solubility series carries is strongly concentrated: the smallest eigenvalue of the
information matrix is five orders of magnitude below the largest. The consequence is quantitative
rather than abstract. No estimator can determine the enthalpy of fusion from such a series to
better than about $\pm 20$~kJ\,mol$^{-1}$, which is wider than the range the quantity takes across
the compounds in the data. Supplying the melting temperature and the enthalpy of fusion as
independent labels reduces that bound by an order of magnitude.

The crystal enthalpy and the temperature slope of the activity coefficient are collinear because
each enters equation~\eqref{eq:sle} through $1/T$. A fit can trade one against the other and
reproduce the measurements equally well. The same argument applies with more force to the $\sigma$-profile, which enters through fifty-one numbers rather than one. A solubility measurement constrains what the profile produces after the closure has acted on it. It leaves the profile itself undetermined in every direction the closure does not expose. What the network places in
those directions is not decided by the data, and the remainder of this section measures what it
places there.

\subsection{The network places charge density where the reference carries none}
\label{sec:windows}

The reference tabulation is sparse by construction. A molecule with no hydrogen-bond donor has no
cavity surface carrying donor-side charge density, and the tabulated profile is exactly zero there.
Across the compounds examined here, well over half of the reference profile's bins are exactly
zero.

The learned profiles are not sparse anywhere. Figure~\ref{fig:windows} shows the donor-window area
for sixteen common solvents. Twelve of the sixteen are exactly empty in that window in the
reference; all sixteen carry area there in the learned profile. The amounts are small as a
fraction of the molecule's surface, between a tenth of a percent and about five percent, and the
hydrogen-bonding term reads them regardless, because a segment qualifies for that term by its
charge density alone.

Where the misfit lands is not uniform across the charge-density axis. Take the residual between the learned and the reference profile, and express it relative to the share of surface area each window holds. The donor window is then over-represented by about a factor of four and the acceptor window by a factor of two, while the nonpolar core is under-represented. The displacement is
therefore oriented: the network's departure from the tabulation concentrates in exactly the regions
that switch on the model's hydrogen-bonding term, and thins in the region that does not.

\subsection{The output layer is not the cause}
\label{sec:pedestal}

An obvious explanation is architectural. The head ends in a normalisation that cannot return an
exact zero, so a profile that should be empty in a bin will instead be slightly populated there,
and the appearance of a defect would be an artefact of the parameterisation. The explanation is
testable, and it fails.

Figure~\ref{fig:pedestal}a shows, for every bin in which the reference is exactly zero, how much
mass the trained head actually places there. The distribution reaches $10^{-11}$, and between a
third and a half of those bins already lie below $10^{-5}$. A layer that can reach $10^{-11}$ is
not what holds the remaining bins at $10^{-5}$. Figure~\ref{fig:pedestal}b makes the same point
from the other direction: an untrained network of the same architecture places about $0.57$ of the
profile's mass in the bins where the reference is empty, and training reduces that to about
$0.02$. The residual population is something training arrives at, not something the parameterisation
imposes.

This matters beyond the present pipeline. A recent physics-embedded model uses a rectified output
with the same normalisation and can therefore emit exact zeros \cite{yang2025tennet}. The
measurement above indicates that the difference between the two output layers is not what decides
whether the learned profile resembles the tabulated one.

\subsection{A molecule is not ideal in itself}
\label{sec:refstate}

Any activity coefficient model must return unity for a pure component: a molecule surrounded by
copies of itself is, by definition, in its reference state. In COSMO-SAC the property holds
identically, because the residual term is a difference between a segment's activity in the mixture
and in the pure compound, and the two coincide when one profile is used on both sides. Supplying a
single profile to both sides of the calculation returns exactly zero here, which confirms that the
implementation carries the property.

The pipeline does not. Because the head reads a role-dependent representation, a compound receives
one profile when it enters as solute and a different one when it enters as solvent. Pairing a
molecule with itself therefore pairs two different profiles, and the activity coefficient departs
from unity. The median departure is a few hundredths in $\ln\gamma$ and the largest exceeds $0.8$.
The violation is a property of the learned intermediate rather than of the thermodynamic relation,
and it is invisible in any evaluation that never pairs a compound with itself, which is every
evaluation on solubility or on mixture data.

\subsection{What misplaced density costs, and where}
\label{sec:cost}

The preceding measurements establish that density sits where the reference says it should not. They
do not say what that costs, and the cost is not uniform. Figure~\ref{fig:costmap}a reports, for
each bin, the mean change in the predicted activity coefficient produced by moving a fixed amount
of mass from the core into that bin. Moving mass within the core is almost free. Moving the same mass into the outer windows is more than an order of magnitude more expensive. The ratio between the two is far larger than it is for random profiles of the same total area, so the structure reflects the chemistry of the exchange rather than the geometry of the grid.

The cost does not grow smoothly with distance along the axis. It is flat across the core, rises
steeply as the hydrogen-bonding threshold is crossed, and then saturates: once mass is inside the
bonding region, pushing it further out changes the prediction comparatively little. The consequence
is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:costmap}b. Reading the measured cost as a potential and taking its
gradient gives the weight under which the usual transport distance between two profiles would price
their difference correctly. That weight peaks at the boundary, not deep in the outer window. A
treatment that weighted the far corner most heavily, which is what the geometry of the grid would
suggest, would weight the wrong place.

\subsection{The intermediate does not decompose}
\label{sec:nonadditive}

If the learned profile were a $\sigma$-profile carrying a per-bin error, then correcting part of it
should help in proportion to the part corrected. It does not. Replacing the learned shape with the
reference shape over the whole grid improves the predicted activity coefficients on every seed.
Replacing it over a randomly chosen third of the bins makes the prediction worse than leaving the profile entirely alone in a fifth of the draws. The outcome ranges from a large improvement to a loss several times the size of the whole gap being closed.

A profile assembled from correct values in some bins and learned values in the rest is not
intermediate between the two. The segment-activity calculation is a self-consistent solution over
the whole distribution, so an internally inconsistent profile can sit far from both of the profiles
it was built from. Two things follow. The first is methodological: the share of the error
attributable to any subset of bins is not defined on this surface, so an analysis that partitions
the grid and attributes error to the parts cannot be carried out, whatever the partition. The
second concerns the premise under test. Partial correctness of the intermediate is not partially
useful; it is frequently worse than consistent incorrectness.

\subsection{The error that reaches the thermodynamics is in the occupied bins}
\label{sec:attribution}

One partition is well posed, because both of its parts are substituted in full: the bins where the
reference is nonzero, and the bins where it is zero. Figure~\ref{fig:attribution}a reports both.
Supplying the reference values in the bins the reference actually occupies recovers close to ninety
percent of the gap between the learned profile and the reference, on every seed. Setting exact
zeros in the bins the reference leaves empty recovers nothing: the mean is slightly negative and
the sign changes from seed to seed, and the result sits inside the spread of a control that zeroes
the same number of randomly chosen cells.

The spurious density is therefore not what the thermodynamics is responding to. The error that
propagates through the closure lives in the magnitude of the profile where the profile is
populated. The cost map of Section~\ref{sec:cost} does not localise this error either. Figure~\ref{fig:attribution}b shows that substituting the reference into the third of the grid the closure is most sensitive to recovers no more of the gap than substituting into a randomly chosen third, and that the sign varies across seeds. Sensitivity per unit of displaced mass and total contribution to the
error are different quantities, and the core carries most of the mass.

Separating the profile's shape from the molecule's cavity area, which COSMO-SAC consumes as a
separate scalar, splits the gap unevenly: about two thirds of it is carried by the shape and the
remainder by the area.
